## 3. Preserve the Architect's adaptive-breach concept

Nathan describes a difficult-to-study entity that repeatedly breaks containment. Researchers deliberately provide environments expected eventually to fail, observe how each breach occurs, and generate new environments in response. The response is not simply disproportionate escalation. Researchers and entity understand their respective positions. The purpose is detailed learning about the escape mechanism for each entity.

Restate this faithfully before analyzing it. Do not reduce the scene to an unwitting attacker trapped in a deceptive honeypot, an endless punishment escalator, or proof of an unbeatable outer cage. Those are different scenarios. Mutual understanding does not by itself establish informed consent, consent to all methods, or freedom to withdraw.

Compare at least three placements: an experimental sector inside the Labyrinth; a separate research facility informing Labyrinth design; and a distinct containment-research concept with only thematic links. Keep all three provisional. Ask whether authorized research-sector breaches count as incidents under the existing RED rule.

Separate a breached test boundary from escape into protected infrastructure or the outside world. An independently protected experimental envelope is a candidate safeguard, not an established feature or an infallible solution. Explicitly investigate the regress: what justifies confidence in the envelope if the entity escapes almost everything? Include an eligibility decision that rejects live testing when adequate bounded risk cannot be justified.

## 4. Research questions and domains

### A. Containment, sandboxing, and AI control

Explain the differences among application sandboxes, containers, virtual machines, microVMs, network isolation, capability restrictions, permissions, monitoring, and organizational controls. Assess the claim that a sandbox is the go-to containment environment: identify contexts in which it is common, alternatives, and reasons no single term establishes an adequate boundary.

Separate prompt injection, unauthorized tool use, data exfiltration, container-to-host escape, hypervisor compromise, benchmark flag retrieval, and genuine uncontrolled external impact. For each reported escape, identify the tested boundary, initial privileges, intentional weaknesses, tools, network access, success criterion, and whether the result generalizes. Avoid sensational claims that AI has escaped all containment or become autonomous outside its infrastructure.

Study AI control evaluations, defense in depth, assumption-of-breach testing, deception environments, adaptive honeynets, system identification, active learning, falsification, chaos engineering, and adversarial co-adaptation. Explain where each analogy breaks down. A honeypot need not involve mutual awareness; chaos engineering normally studies system response to faults rather than a strategic captive.

### B. Experimental environments and failure-informed learning

Define physical, simulated, informational, hybrid, and speculative dimensional environments. Compare static controls, random changes, adaptive changes, and deliberate non-escalatory variation. Specify which parameters change, which remain fixed, and what observation would distinguish competing explanations for an escape.

Address observability, telemetry integrity, measurement interference, missing events, causal inference, repeated-trial dependence, evaluation awareness, and strategic underperformance. The entity may teach researchers a false explanation, learn the experimenter, or escape through the observer or generator rather than the test boundary.

### C. Entity scope and admission criteria

Build a provisional taxonomy spanning biological, synthetic, AI, distributed, collective, embodied, informational, and setting-specific entities. Separate evidenced real-world categories from fictional categories. Assess whether a candidate can be safely observed at all. Do not infer universal susceptibility from a single energy-absorption scene or a catalogued survival record.

Distinguish punitive prisoners, protective custody, quarantine, voluntary research participants, involuntary research subjects, and non-sentient test systems. Define evidence, necessity, proportionality, jurisdiction, care, review, and uncertainty for each. Capability is not guilt; admission to prison is not authorization for experimentation.

## 5. Adversarial experimental design requirements

Specify hypotheses before candidate solutions. For every preferred option give its strongest supporting evidence, strongest objection, assumptions, failure condition, alternative, and observation that would change the recommendation. Include a no-live-test alternative.

Design only a conceptual authorized protocol: define research question and boundary; assess eligibility and consent; specify controls; establish independent observation; run bounded trials; preserve evidence; analyze causality; propose adaptation; review it before deployment; compare against held-out cases; stop or reassess when necessary. Do not provide exploit payloads, real escape instructions, or instructions to expose live third-party systems.

Measure learning quality rather than simply longer containment time. Candidate measures include causal explanation confidence, reproducibility, prediction on held-out cases, detection delay, false alarms, intervention burden, care continuity, computational cost, and unauthorized cross-boundary events. Explain why observed zero events do not prove zero risk. Predefine stop conditions without inventing unsupported numerical thresholds.

Address dual learning: improving the entity's escape ability may outpace improving containment. Examine poisoning of telemetry, misleading demonstrations, common-mode faults, untrusted generated environments, hidden communication paths, observer contamination, resource exhaustion, participant distress, and irreversibility. Adaptation must not automatically mean harsher treatment.
