# Part 2 — LOIE-CLAUDE-02: Expected-Breach Series Model

## 2.1 What the model must satisfy

Your scene, restated: a very hard-to-contain entity is placed in a container built to eventually fail. Researchers watch how it breaches. A new containment environment is generated in response. The cycle repeats. Both sides understand their positions. [Architect Direction]

The model must hold at the same time:

- breach is expected and foundational;
- the Labyrinth is inescapable;
- responses do not simply get harder;
- breach is studied in detail;
- no one is deceived about the arrangement.

## 2.2 Breach classes

Treating all breaches as one thing is the fastest way to let an expected failure become a real one. I separate seven classes.

1. **Test-cell breach.** The entity leaves the cell it was placed in. Expected.
2. **Sector breach.** It crosses into a neighboring test cell or shared service. Unwanted but tolerable if the sector is itself disposable.
3. **Custody breach.** It affects staff, records or the people responsible for holding it.
4. **Exterior breach.** It reaches the outer envelope. This is the one canon says must not happen.
5. **Information breach.** Data about the design, the staff, or other inmates leaves, even if the entity does not.
6. **Identity or copy breach.** A copy, a proxy or an impersonation acts outside while the original stays inside.
7. **Authorized release.** Someone with authority lets it out, by order, coercion or error.

[Fictional Mechanism Proposal] Only class 1 is designed to happen. Classes 2 and 5 are monitored. Classes 3, 4, 6 and 7 count as failures of the system, not as experimental results.

## 2.3 Three placement options

**Placement A: inside the Labyrinth.** The series runs in a quarantined region of the infinite interior.
- Strength: uses the existing structure and keeps entities from ever being outside it.
- Weakness: a test breach is then a breach inside the prison, which blurs the line between a designed failure and a real one. It also gives the entity a path toward the structure's management surface.
- Effect on canon: strains "inescapable," since the prison is now routinely breached from within.

**Placement B: alongside the Labyrinth.** A separate facility, linked to it, serves as a proving ground. Entities that pass through the series may later be admitted to the Labyrinth.
- Strength: keeps the Labyrinth's inescapability claim clean. Breaches happen elsewhere.
- Weakness: it is then a second prison with its own outer boundary, and its outer boundary has no guarantee.
- Effect on canon: preserves Entry 26 and relocates the problem.

**Placement C: apart from the Labyrinth.** The series is independent, with its own staff and records. It may feed information to the Labyrinth's designers but never admits to it.
- Strength: strongest separation of study from sentencing.
- Weakness: highest cost, and a duplicate set of custodians.
- Effect on canon: silent, which means it needs a stated relationship.

My lean, which is a recommendation and not a decision, is Placement B, because it protects the inescapability claim while keeping the series close enough to inform the design. It depends on your answer to Question 1 in Part 9. [Fictional Mechanism Proposal]

## 2.4 The regress problem

The outer envelope is the thing that must never fail. What justifies confidence in it? If the answer is "it was tested," then it was a test cell. If the answer is "it was never tested," confidence rests on design reasoning alone, and the 2026 incidents show design reasoning about isolation is often wrong. [Researched Finding, Analogy]

Three ways to live with this, none of which removes it:

- **Defense in depth with unlike layers.** Layers that fail for different reasons. [Analogy: Reason's layered-defense model, from general knowledge, not looked up here. Unverified.]
- **An envelope that is simple enough to reason about.** Simplicity is itself a security property. The infinite interior is complicated. The envelope should not be. [Fictional Mechanism Proposal]
- **A no-live-test alternative.** Test the envelope only against models and simulations, never against a live, hard-to-contain entity. The cost is lower confidence. The benefit is that a test never doubles as an escape. [Fictional Mechanism Proposal]

## 2.5 Six-iteration study

This is a design study, not a manual for defeating anything. Breach mechanisms are described by class and consequence, never by method. Each iteration lists what happens and what the researchers change. Entity descriptions are generic placeholders and make no claim about any canon entity.

**Iteration 1: the first cell fails as predicted.** A test-cell breach, class 1. Researchers record what was crossed and how long it took. Response: they rebuild the cell with one changed variable and keep the rest, so they can tell which change mattered. Lesson: change one thing at a time.

**Iteration 2: the entity breaches faster than predicted.** Class 1, but the timeline collapses. Researchers cannot tell whether the cell was weak or the entity stronger than modeled. Response: they do not harden. They add instrumentation to separate those two explanations. This iteration is *confounded by entity learning*. The entity may have learned from iteration 1, which breaks the assumption that each trial is independent.

**Iteration 3: the response gets simpler.** The researchers discover that two of the cell's layers were never used by the entity, and removing them changes nothing. The next cell is smaller. Lesson: failure can reduce complexity, which counters the habit of adding walls. This is the *gets simpler* case.

**Iteration 4: the failure comes through staff.** A class 3 event. A staff member, through fatigue, trust, pressure or error, becomes the path. Not a technical breach. Response: the fix is a change in staffing, shifts and separation of duties, not in the cell. This is the *fails via staff or trust* case. It is the most realistic, given that two of the three 2026 failure modes were configuration and permission errors, not novel exploits. [Analogy]

**Iteration 5: a false escape.** The entity appears to breach and is later found never to have left. Or the researchers believe it is still contained and it is not. Either way the observation is unreliable. Response: a verification step that does not depend on the entity's own behavior or on a single observer. Lesson: *feigned failure* is a possible entity strategy, and the loop must not treat a breach signal as ground truth. [Analogy: the sandbagging and deceptive-evaluation concerns in the AI control literature; I have not verified specific papers for this point.]

**Iteration 6: stop condition reached.** The series ends not because containment succeeded but because a pre-set criterion was met: the marginal information per iteration fell below a threshold, or a class 3, 4, 6 or 7 breach occurred, or the entity's participation no longer met the consent standard (§2.6). Lesson: a series with no stop condition is an arms race with paperwork.

## 2.7 Hypotheses H1 to H3 assessed

**H1 (breach-expected design is safer).** Mixed. For *test cells*, yes: designing for failure lets you control where it happens. For the *outer envelope*, no: expecting failure there makes it a design goal to fail. The honest answer is "safer in the interior, not at the boundary." What would change my mind: evidence that planned failure at a boundary can be reliably reversed.

**H2 (studying breaches converges rather than arms-races).** Not supported by anything I found. The AI-control and sandbox literature, as far as I read, assumes an adversarial dynamic and measures protocols against it. It does not show convergence. Iteration 2 shows the risk directly. I hold H2 as *unproven and plausibly false*. What would change my mind: a case where defender learning measurably outpaced attacker learning over repeated trials.

**H3 (breach distinguishable from escape).** Partly. [Researched Finding] So detection of a real escape may depend on evidence from outside the boundary. Inside a closed system, that evidence may not exist. I rate H3 as *weak support, low confidence*.

# Part 3 — LOIE-CLAUDE-03: Containment Taxonomy, Criteria and Intake Decision Tree

## 3.1 Who or what can be contained

Capability to be contained is not the same as justification for it. This section lists what *can* be, not what *should* be.

1. **Individual entities.** Persons, ET beings, constructs, engineered beings. [Canon-adjacent; Entry 26 implies inmates but names no classes]
2. **Collectives.** A coalition, a caste, a fleet. [Canon: the prior Founding coalition was contained, Entry 6]
3. **Artifacts.** Absorbed technology, weapons, objects carrying intent or capability. This is the S5 case.
4. **Information and copies.** A record, a model, a backup, an impersonation.
5. **Processes.** A running routine, a self-propagating mechanism.
6. **Locations or regions.** A sector, a dimension (compare Entry 65, Boundary Dimension).

[Fictional Mechanism Proposal] The category matters because the right container, the right review and the right ethics differ. Containing an artifact is not imprisoning a person. Treating them the same is the most common way for a containment system to drift into abuse.

## 3.2 Two tracks, kept apart

This follows hypothesis H8, which I hold as strongly supported by the real-world material: capability alone never justifies punishment.

**Protective containment.** Grounded in risk. Aimed at preventing future harm. No punitive conditions. Bounded review cadence. Ends when the risk ends.

**Punitive containment (a sentence).** Grounded in a proven act and the agent's responsibility for it. Aimed at accountability. May be long. Still subject to review and to correction if wrong.

The Labyrinth in canon reads as punitive and unbounded. The design question is whether it also holds protective cases. If it does, those inmates need a visibly different regime, or the system punishes people for what they could do. [Open Question — AUTHOR INPUT REQUIRED: does the Labyrinth hold both?]

## 3.3 Criteria for containment (pre-registered for Part 4)

These were written before the test and not edited afterward.

- **G1. Act.** A real act or a credible, imminent one. Not status, rank, species, origin or association.
- **G2. Severity.** Serious harm that is irreversible or at scale.
- **G3. Attribution.** The act is attributed to this agent. Identity is verified. Not spoofed, coerced or possessed.
- **G4. Necessity.** Lesser measures failed, or are shown to be unavailable.
- **G5. Legitimate decider.** The decider is not a party to the dispute.
- **G6. Review and reversal.** A review path exists, and a release path exists that is not an escape.

**Punitive track requires:** G1, G2, G3, G5, G6, plus responsibility for the act.
**Protective track requires:** a credible risk assessment, G4, G5 and G6, with a fixed review interval.

**Evidence standards [Fictional Mechanism Proposal]:** punitive needs clear and convincing evidence of both act and attribution. Protective needs a risk assessment reviewed by someone independent of the person who requested it.

**The role-swap rule.** A criterion counts only if applying it to the same facts, with the roles swapped, gives the same verdict. A criterion that does not is a verdict in disguise. (This is hypothesis H7.)

## 3.4 Sentencing

Canon: the sentence would not "mark a dent" if time ended. [Canon] I read that as describing scale, not necessarily a literal no-release rule. [Open Question; I do not settle it.]

Proposal: separate *duration* from *review*. A sentence may be unbounded in length and still have fixed review points and a defined release path. Real-world standards treat indefinite isolation as prohibited for human prisoners. [Researched Finding] Whether the Labyrinth's inmates are covered by anything similar is a design decision, and the design should say so openly.

## 3.5 Humane-standards tension (adversarial point)

The Mandela Rules prohibit three practices relevant here. [Researched Finding]

- Indefinite solitary confinement.
- Prolonged solitary confinement, meaning more than 15 consecutive days.
- Placement in a dark or constantly lit cell.

Entry 26's persistent white glow is, on its face, a constantly lit environment. Its unbounded sentence is, on its face, indefinite. Whether inmates are in *solitary* depends on the layout, which is OPEN. So the tension is conditional, not certain. But it is the kind of thing a reader familiar with real prison standards will notice first.

Options for you to choose between:

- **Acknowledge and accept.** The Labyrinth is intentionally beyond those norms, and the story says so.
- **Acknowledge and mitigate.** Constant light is a design necessity for monitoring, and the inmates get contact, activity and review.
- **Decline the analogy.** The inmates are not covered by human norms. The design says why.

All three are legitimate. The risky choice is silence.

## 3.6 Intake decision tree (as numbered steps)

1. Is there an identified entity, artifact, copy or process? If not, stop. Nothing to contain.
2. Is identity verified to the standard in G3? If not, do not contain the person. Contain the access path or the artifact, and re-verify.
3. Is there an act or credible imminent act (G1)? If no, go to step 8.
4. Is the harm serious, irreversible or at scale (G2)? If no, use a lesser measure, then stop.
5. Have lesser measures failed or been shown unavailable (G4)? If no, use them.
6. Is the decider a party to the dispute (G5)? If yes, recuse and refer to the alternate decider. [Open: who]
7. Is the agent responsible for the act? If yes, punitive track with review and release path (G6). If no, protective track.
8. No act: is there a credible, independently reviewed risk assessment? If yes, protective track only, with a short review interval. If no, do not contain.
9. At every step, record who decided, on what evidence, and what would reverse it.

# Part 4 — LOIE-CLAUDE-04: Foundational Architect Containment Test

## 4.1 What this test is, and is not

It tests *criteria*, not guilt. The scenarios are hypothetical. They are not claims about what any Founding Architect did, and they do not assert that any Founding Architect belongs in the Labyrinth. [Canon: Entry 6 leaves that open]

## 4.2 Limits of this test (read first)

1. **One reviewer.** I applied the criteria alone. The prompt called for independent blind application by each AI with disagreements logged in AIDR. That has not happened.
2. **Not blind.** I wrote the criteria after reading the scenario battery. I tried not to tailor them, but I cannot rule it out.
3. **No ground truth.** In fiction there is no fact of the matter about the right verdict. The test measures consistency and symmetry, not correctness.
4. **Role-swap is my judgment.** I decided what "swapped" means in each case. Others may disagree.

## 4.3 Results, scenario by scenario

For each: the criteria used, evidence standard, decider, verdict, swapped verdict, and reversibility.

**S1. An Architect uses their architecture to cause serious harm.**
Criteria: G1 to G6. Evidence: clear and convincing, act and attribution. Decider: neutral, not a party. Verdict: punitive containment justified. Swapped: same verdict. Reversible: partly, if the review path works. **Stable.**

**S2. An Architect refuses a valid process but has done no harm.**
Criteria: G1 fails. Refusal is not an act of harm. Verdict: no containment. Swapped: same. Catch: "valid process" must be defined by someone, and Entry 15 says authority is recognized, not granted. If the Architect defines validity, the test is circular. **Needs an explicit rule: process validity must be set independently of the person it is applied to.**

**S3. An Architect tries to reclaim a position by force.**
Criteria: G1 met; G2 depends on the harm done. Verdict: at least protective containment; punitive if harm occurred. Swapped: the criteria apply equally to anyone who takes a position by force. This is where canon bites. Entry 6 says the current Architect's position came from a seizure. I do not know the facts of that seizure and I do not infer them. What I can say is that these criteria, applied consistently, would reach that act if it meets G1 and G2, and that whether the prior order was legitimate is the missing piece. **Needs an explicit rule: a baseline for when an existing order is legitimate. [OPEN — AUTHOR INPUT REQUIRED]**

**S4. An Architect tries to reclaim a position by a lawful process.**
G1 fails. A political act is not an offense. Verdict: no containment. Swapped: same. Catch: it assumes a lawful process exists. **Stable, given that one exists.**

**S5. An Architect's absorbed technology may carry residual active intent, but the person has done nothing.**

# Part 5 — LOIE-CLAUDE-05: Identity, Access, Control and Governance Options

## 5.1 Design principles drawn from the real world

- **Network location alone does not imply trust.** From NIST SP 800-207. Applied here: being inside the Labyrinth, or holding a staff badge, must not by itself grant access. [Researched Finding, Analogy]
- **Policy decisions and enforcement are separate components.** Also from 800-207. [Researched Finding] Separating who decides from who enforces limits what one compromise gives an attacker.
- **Safety and availability constrain security.** From the SP 800-82 line of guidance on operational technology. [Researched Finding, secondary] A control that endangers life support is not an acceptable control.
- **Statements are not controls.** From the 2026 incidents. [Researched Finding]
- **Two-person integrity** for the most dangerous actions. [Analogy from nuclear-weapons practice, general knowledge, not looked up here. Unverified.]

## 5.2 Six control planes (hypothesis H4)

A control plane is a set of functions that can be compromised independently.

1. Confinement.
2. Life support and environment.
3. Identity.
4. Authority.
5. Records.
6. Oversight.

[Fictional Mechanism Proposal] The aim is that no one failure reaches all six. The real-world lesson is the weak point: planes that look separate often share a dependency. In the 2026 reports, a single permitted exit path was the route out. [Researched Finding, secondary] Any design that lists six planes should also list their shared services and treat each as a single point of failure.

**H4 assessed:** plausible, and only as strong as its shared-dependency audit. Confidence: low to moderate.

## 5.3 Identity and the authority tension

Entry 15 says authority is recognized, not granted. [Canon] That has a consequence for security: if authority follows recognition, then forging *recognition* forges authority. Identity theft becomes authority theft. This is the S9 problem.

Proposal [Fictional Mechanism Proposal]: separate *identity* (who this is) from *authorization* (what this identity may do now). Identity is slow, verified, multi-source. Authorization is narrow, short-lived and revocable. The Architect's authority is canon, but the *act-level* authorization to open, close or release anything can still be layered without contradicting it.

## 5.4 Three competing architectures, each red-teamed

### Architecture A: Sovereign

The Architect holds root authority. Warden tiers hold delegated authority. A single chain of command.

**Strengths:** fast decisions, a clear chain, a close match to Entry 15 and positional sovereignty in Entry 7.

**Red team:**
- Single point of failure. Compromise, coercion or spoofing of the sovereign compromises everything (S9).
- The sovereign is a party to disputes, with no recusal (S7).
- Wrongful containment has no correction route outside the sovereign's will (S6).
- Subordinates cannot refuse an unsound order without risk to themselves.
- It is brittle: it works until the root is lost.

### Architecture B: Treaty-governed

The Architect, the staff polity and an oversight body share power under a charter. Critical actions need agreement between parties.

**Strengths:** accountability, recusal built in, correction routes, legitimacy with staff.

**Red team:**
- Deadlock in an emergency.
- The charter's own legitimacy: who wrote it, who can amend it.
- Collusion between two parties defeats the check.
- Slower response where speed matters.
- Staff polity may have interests that diverge from the Architect's, and canon is silent.

### Architecture C: Distributed custodianship

No one holds all keys. Critical actions need a quorum drawn from separated roles. Control planes are held by different groups.

**Strengths:** resilient to loss of any one person, aligned with separation of planes (H4), resistant to a single coercion.

**Red team:**
- Coordination cost and error. More people and more handoffs.
- Quorum capture by a compromised minority, if the quorum is small.
- Accountability diffuses; when everyone is responsible, no one is.
- Complexity is itself an attack surface. This is the Normal Accidents concern. [Analogy: Perrow, general knowledge, not looked up here. Unverified.]
- Cuts against the canonical, sovereign-centered feel of Entries 7 and 15.

### 5.5 Comparison and recommendation

No architecture wins on all failure types. A cleaner view is by *plane*:

- For **confinement and life support**, distributed custodianship (C) fits best, because these are the planes where a single compromise is most dangerous.
- For **oversight and release**, treaty-style governance (B) fits best, because these need independence from the operator.
- For **final authority**, the Architect's sovereignty (A) can remain, if it cannot unilaterally perform the most dangerous or irreversible actions without a second party.

This hybrid is my recommendation. It is a proposal and not a decision. It preserves Entry 15 and Entry 7 while giving S6, S7 and S9 a route. [Fictional Mechanism Proposal]

**Assessed:** partly. A bounded surface helps. But it implies that most of the interior is not directly observed, and the design must say so honestly. Entry 73 (MVES) is described by peers as guaranteeing complete mapping. I could not support that claim, because completeness over an infinite space is not demonstrated by anything in canon. ## 5.7 Sentence without end (H6)

**Assessed:** conditionally supported. # Part 8 — LOIE-CLAUDE-08: Failure Taxonomy and Adversarial Scenario Register

## 8.1 Failure taxonomy

1. **Technical.** A boundary or mechanism does not do what was assumed.
2. **Configuration.** A gap or permission left open by mistake. In the 2026 reports this was common. [Researched Finding]
3. **Human and staff.** Fatigue, trust, pressure, error, coercion.
4. **Governance and legitimacy.** Capture, deadlock, a decider who is a party.
5. **Information.** Leakage of design, staff or inmate data.
6. **Identity.** Spoofing, impersonation, copies.
7. **Ethical and consent.** Wrongful admission, uncorrectable error, pressure presented as consent.
8. **Epistemic.** Believing something is true that is not: false escape, unobserved regions, false assurance.
9. **Environmental and life support.** Staff or inmates harmed by conditions.
10. **Downstream.** Successors, copies and consequences that outlive the event.

## 8.2 Adversarial scenario register

Each is a conceptual scenario for design review. None describes methods. For each: the class, what fails, the early signal, and the design response.

1. **The mundane gap.** Class 2. A path assumed closed is open. Signal: unexplained traffic or presence outside the expected region. Response: default-deny and an independent exit audit. Based on the 2026 pattern. [Analogy]
2. **The instruction that was only an instruction.** Class 2 and 8. Everyone relies on a rule the entity was told, with nothing enforcing it. Signal: reliance on "it was told not to." Response: replace statements with enforced limits.
3. **Shared dependency.** Class 1. Two "separate" control planes rely on one service. Signal: dependency map shows overlap. Response: split or duplicate the service.
4. **The tired warden.** Class 3. A long shift leads to a skipped check. Signal: rising error rate late in rotation. Response: shorter shifts, second-person checks.
5. **The sympathetic staff member.** Class 3. Staff form a bond with an inmate and act on it. Signal: unusual contact patterns. Response: contact rules, rotation, a safe way to report concerns.
6. **False escape.** Class 8. The entity seems to breach and has not, drawing resources away. Signal: breach signal without corroboration. Response: verification independent of the signal.
7. **Feigned failure to learn the researchers.** Class 8. The entity "fails" to study the response. Signal: breach pattern that gives away little. Response: treat each breach as possibly informative to both sides, and vary responses.
8. **Researcher capture.** Class 4 and 7. The study becomes the goal and the series continues past its useful point. Signal: no stop condition met, yet marginal information is falling. Response: pre-set stop rules (§2.5, iteration 6).
9. **Sovereign compromised.** Class 6. The root authority is spoofed or coerced (S9). Signal: orders inconsistent with prior conduct. Response: dual authorization on irreversible actions.
10. **The party as judge.** Class 4. The Architect decides a case in which they are involved (S7). Signal: no recusal recorded. Response: a recusal rule and alternate decider.
11. **Wrongful admission found too late.** Class 7. An innocent entity is found to have been admitted wrongly (S6). Signal: new evidence. Response: an authorized release path that is not an escape.
12. **Constant light harms.** Class 7 and 9. Prolonged constant illumination causes harm, as the Mandela Rule 43 concern suggests for humans. Signal: inmate deterioration. Response: a stated standard, monitoring, and mitigation if the setting adopts one.
13. **Funder influence.** Class 4. A payer pushes for admissions or releases. Signal: decisions correlate with funding events. Response: separate funding from decision.
14. **Unobserved region.** Class 8. Something occurs in a part of the infinite interior that nothing observes. Signal: absence of data treated as absence of events. Response: state the unobserved assumption and design for it (§5.6).
15. **Information leaves, entity stays.** Class 5. The design, rotas or inmate data leak. Signal: external knowledge of internal facts. Response: compartmentalization and need-to-know.
16. **Copy outside, original inside.** Class 6. A proxy acts outside while the original is contained. Signal: activity attributed to a contained entity. Response: treat copies and proxies as part of the containment problem.
17. **Successor that cannot be contained.** Class 10. A creation exceeds the container (S10). Signal: capability exceeds assessments. Response: protective track only, with review, and no punishment for capability alone (H8).
18. **Absorbed technology with residual intent.** Class 10. Technology absorbed from the prior coalition acts on its own (S5, FA-Q8). Signal: unexplained behavior in absorbed systems. Response: needs a neutral decider; unresolved. [OPEN]
19. **Authorized release under pressure.** Class 4 and 7. Someone with authority releases an inmate under coercion or error. Signal: out-of-pattern release order. Response: two-person integrity and a delay on release.
20. **Staff area becomes a dependency.** Class 3 and 9. A failure in life support for staff forces evacuation, leaving custody unattended. Signal: staff-area fault. Response: separate the planes and keep custody continuity independent.

- Studying an entity is not the same as sentencing it. The design should keep these separate.
- I applied the Foundational Architect test alone. Treat it as a baseline.
- Several real-world claims rest on secondary or aggregator sources and one primary page could not be read. They are labelled.
- Some analogies, such as honeynets, chaos engineering, Reason's layered-defense model, Perrow's normal accidents and two-person integrity, come from general knowledge and were not looked up here. They are labelled Unverified.
- I am an Anthropic model writing about incidents involving Anthropic's models and rivals' models. I tried to be even-handed.
- Nothing here is canon until you decide.
