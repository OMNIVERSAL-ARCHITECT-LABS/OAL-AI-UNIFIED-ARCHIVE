# Project-Owner Direction on Conflicting AI Recommendations and Simulation-Lab Autonomy

## Purpose

This response clarifies how approved but conflicting recommendations from AI collaborators are to be handled within the Transmogrification Framework II project. A conflict between approved recommendations does not require one recommendation to be erased, subordinated, or treated as a mistake. Instead, the conflict may become the basis for parallel simulations, separately defined laboratory branches, and comparative evaluation.

The governing principle is **coordinated pluralism**: maintain enough shared structure to compare results, while preserving enough laboratory autonomy to test genuinely different assumptions, methods, rules, and outcomes.

## Revised Direction

### Governing Principle

Where two or more AI collaborators have submitted conflicting recommendations and those recommendations have both been approved, the approvals shall be interpreted as authorization to test multiple approaches—not as an instruction to force the recommendations into a single design.

Each AI collaborator may therefore develop and run its own laboratory, simulation branch, scenario family, or model implementation to test its recommendation independently. For example, a Copilot Lab does not invalidate or interfere with a Grok Lab merely because the two use different assumptions, structures, variables, constraints, or evaluation criteria. Both may proceed, and their differing results should be preserved for comparison.

No collaborator should treat another collaborator’s approved laboratory design as a restriction on its own approved work unless the project owner explicitly establishes such a restriction. Approval of one approach does not silently revoke approval of another.

### Productive Conflict

Differences among collaborators are expected and valuable. The purpose of involving multiple AI systems is not to make every collaborator produce the same proposal, run the same scenario, apply the same assumptions, or reach the same conclusion.

Conflicting recommendations should be treated as competing hypotheses or alternative experimental branches. The project should make space for:

- Different laboratory architectures.
- Different interpretations of the same problem.
- Different assumptions about system behavior.
- Different rule sets and simulated environments.
- Different proposed solutions and failure models.
- Different outcomes, including negative, inconclusive, or unexpected outcomes.
- Direct criticism, rebuttal, and comparison among approaches.

The existence of disagreement is not, by itself, a defect to be removed. It can reveal hidden assumptions, expose boundary conditions, identify untested alternatives, and produce new questions.

### Baseline and Counterfactual Labs

The project should distinguish between **baseline simulations** and **counterfactual simulations** rather than allowing present-day constraints to end the inquiry.

A baseline lab should use established physical laws, current engineering limits, documented system behavior, and realistic constraints. Its purpose is to determine what appears feasible under present conditions.

A counterfactual lab may deliberately modify, suspend, invert, or replace one or more assumptions or simulated laws to explore how the system would behave under different conditions. The purpose is not to claim that altered laws apply in the real world; it is to investigate consequences, dependencies, limits, and alternative possibilities inside a clearly labeled simulation.

Accordingly, “the laws of physics do not permit this under current conditions” may be a valid conclusion for a baseline branch, but it should not automatically prohibit a separate counterfactual branch from asking:

- Which law or constraint prevents the result?
- Why does that constraint matter?
- What changes if the constraint is modified?
- Where are the constraint’s limits or boundary conditions?
- What secondary consequences appear when the rule changes?
- Does changing the rule solve the original problem or merely relocate its cost?
- What new risks, dependencies, or failure modes emerge?

The simulation environment is a laboratory for questioning assumptions, not merely a mechanism for reproducing conclusions already accepted in advance.

### Anti-Confirmation Requirement

Laboratories should not be designed only to validate a preferred conclusion. Each lab should make a deliberate effort to test where its proposal fails, what evidence would disconfirm it, and whether an alternative model explains the scenario more effectively.

When practical, each laboratory should include several kinds of runs:

- A baseline run using the lab’s default assumptions.
- An alternative-assumption run.
- A boundary or stress test.
- A failure or reversal test.
- A disconfirmation run designed to challenge the lab’s preferred hypothesis.
- A cross-lab comparison using at least some shared inputs or metrics.

A simulation that can only confirm its starting premise is not sufficiently exploratory. Unexpected, adverse, contradictory, and null results should be retained rather than discarded.

### Shared Reporting, Not Uniform Thinking

Laboratory autonomy does not require analytical isolation. To make different results comparable, each lab should document:

- The recommendation or conflict that prompted the lab.
- The lab owner and version.
- The research question or hypothesis.
- The assumptions used.
- The laws, constraints, or rules retained.
- Any laws, constraints, or rules modified or suspended.
- The reason for each material modification.
- The scenario inputs, variables, and initial conditions.
- The model, code, configuration, and version used.
- The success criteria and evaluation metrics.
- The results, including failures and inconclusive findings.
- Known limitations, uncertainty, and blind spots.
- Differences from other approved labs.
- Follow-up questions and recommended next experiments.

The common framework should serve as a coordination and comparison layer. It may standardize naming, provenance, versioning, minimum documentation, and safety controls without requiring every laboratory to use the same hypotheses or produce the same findings.

### Evolving Code and Labs

Code, models, assumptions, and scenario libraries are not static. As the project evolves, laboratories should be revised, forked, rerun, and compared across versions.

Each material change should be versioned so collaborators can determine whether an altered outcome resulted from a new assumption, a changed rule, a code revision, a different input, or a newly discovered limitation. Earlier results should remain in the project history rather than being silently overwritten.

A previous laboratory result is evidence from one configuration at one point in development; it is not an eternal rule preventing later experimentation.

### Conflict Procedure

When a conflict is identified between approved recommendations, use the following procedure:

1. Record the conflicting recommendations and the workbook items in which they appear.
2. Confirm that both recommendations remain approved or note any explicit modification.
3. Create separate named branches or labs when reconciliation would erase a meaningful difference.
4. Identify any shared inputs or metrics needed for a useful comparison.
5. Allow each lab to preserve its own assumptions, methods, and proposed mechanics.
6. Run baseline, counterfactual, stress, and disconfirmation tests where appropriate.
7. Report results without forcing consensus or concealing disagreement.
8. Compare what each approach reveals, including strengths, limitations, tradeoffs, and new questions.
9. Escalate only those conflicts that prevent implementation, create an unresolved safety issue, or require an explicit project-owner decision.

### Limits of Autonomy

Experimental freedom does not remove the need for clear labeling, provenance, ethical controls, legal compliance, or safety boundaries. A counterfactual result must not be presented as an empirical real-world result, and speculative mechanics must not be mislabeled as presently feasible engineering.

These safeguards protect the integrity of the comparison; they are not intended to suppress creative or unconventional experimentation.

## Copy-Paste Approval Block

**Additional project-owner direction regarding conflicting recommendations and simulation-lab autonomy**

Where recommendations from different AI collaborators conflict and both recommendations have been approved, neither approval should be interpreted as automatically canceling, overriding, or restricting the other. Instead, the conflicting recommendations may proceed as separate simulation branches or independently designed laboratories.

Each AI collaborator is authorized to test its own approved approach. A Copilot Lab may use a different structure, rule set, assumption set, or method from a Grok Lab, Gemini Lab, ChatGPT Lab, Claude Lab, or Perplexity Lab. These differences are permitted and expected. The purpose of multi-AI collaboration is not to force every model to propose the same design, run the same scenario, or reach the same outcome. Different points of view, conflicting hypotheses, negative findings, and divergent outcomes must be allowed to remain visible.

The shared framework should coordinate terminology, documentation, versioning, provenance, comparison metrics, and safety requirements; it should not impose uniform thinking. Where reconciliation would erase a meaningful difference, create parallel named branches and compare their results instead.

Simulation labs must also avoid confirmation-only design. They should not run scenarios solely to reproduce conclusions already assumed to be correct. Each lab should test alternatives, limits, failure conditions, reversals, and disconfirming cases. Baseline labs may apply established physical laws and current engineering constraints, while clearly labeled counterfactual labs may modify selected assumptions or simulated laws to investigate what changes, why it changes, and what new consequences emerge. Counterfactual results must remain clearly distinguished from claims about real-world feasibility.

Code, models, and simulation environments are not static. They can be revised, forked, and evolved over time. Laboratory designs should therefore be versioned and rerun as assumptions, code, data, or research questions change. Earlier results should be preserved for comparison rather than treated as permanent limits on future inquiry.

For every separate laboratory branch, document the question being tested, assumptions, retained and altered rules, inputs, model or code version, metrics, results, failures, uncertainty, limitations, and differences from other branches. The objective is not artificial consensus. The objective is disciplined exploration: think differently, test differently, compare honestly, and remain open to outcomes that no collaborator predicted in advance.
