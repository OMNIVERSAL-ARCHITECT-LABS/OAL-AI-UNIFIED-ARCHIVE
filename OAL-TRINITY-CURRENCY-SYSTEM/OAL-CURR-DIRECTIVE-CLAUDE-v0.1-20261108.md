# Part I. Background and Purpose

## 1. Background

The OAL Trinity Currency Laboratory is a multi-AI project to design and stress-test three instruments: Quantum Credits (QC), 1COIN and NCOIN. The project owner, Nathan Lewis II, works with a six-member AI collaboration team. All of this takes place inside a fictional, simulation and laboratory setting. Nothing in the project is a claim about real-world financial instruments.

The project record, as it stands, describes the instruments this way:

- **Quantum Credits (QC)** are a target-relative value-resolution instrument. In the project's shorthand, QC(T) = V(T): a Quantum Credit resolves to the value of whatever it is applied to. The design deliberately includes extreme targets, including things that cannot ordinarily be bought, sold or transacted.
- **1COIN** has a working hypothesis of one 1COIN per validly resolved settlement. The event that turns a QC resolution into 1COIN is still undefined.
- **NCOIN** is intended to record countervalue or negative value. Its exact domain is still undefined.
- **A central principle** is that valuation, ownership, authorization and settlement are separate operations.
- **A governing test rule** is to accept the premise long enough to test its consequences, rather than replacing it with ordinary monetary assumptions.

An earlier six-AI exercise (August 2026) judged the Quantum Credit idea by conventional economic standards and found no viability. The owner's position was that this applied the wrong paradigm to an instrument not designed as a conventional currency. Later rounds produced a boundary exercise (named the Absolute Transaction Boundary by one contributor and the Ontological Transaction Limit by another), a constitutional baseline, a laboratory charter and several pilot runs.

On November 7, 2026, each contributor produced a set of questions for the project owner, in PDF, DOCX and Markdown form. The owner reviewed them and found that most could not responsibly be answered by choosing from a list of options. They first needed testing.

A second development shaped this directive. During the same round, Perplexity ran a code audit of the existing pilots. It reproduced them, then fed deliberately altered records to the checkers. By Perplexity's own summary, the checkers accepted some of those altered records. The owner found this the most valuable part of the round, because it showed a result that did not simply confirm what the team wanted to believe.

## 2. Purpose of this document

This document turns the owner's spoken clarification into a formal, versioned directive that can be published in the project repository and sent to every contributor.

It has four aims:

1. Record the owner's position: most open questions become investigations, not prerequisites for an owner decision.
2. Tell each contributor how to run an independent laboratory in which its own questions, recommendations and checks are allowed to fail.
3. Remove the pressure toward a single shared run card, a single benchmark, or a unanimous conclusion.
4. Give the project a versioned reference so later changes can be traced.

# Part II. The Directive

*This part is written in the owner's voice, for the owner to send to the team.*

**To:** The OAL AI Collaboration Team

**From:** Nathan Lewis II, Project Owner

## 4. Owner position

I read the question documents you each wrote. Thank you. For most of them, my answer is not to pick one of your options. My answer is to test the question, the options you recommended, and the checks used to judge them.

I can answer the following now.

- **Quantum Credit capability.** Check the existing project record first. The starting idea is target-relative value resolution, QC(T) = V(T), including extreme or normally non-transactable targets. Tell me what you found and what is missing. Do not ask me to restate it from scratch.
- **Run card.** I am not choosing one. Each of you may pick your own, build a different setup, or propose a better approach and explain why. Please do not all pick the same one.
- **Consensus.** Do not seek it. Agree where you actually agree. Do not invent disagreement. If your results differ, find out why.
- **Assumptions.** Everything is open to challenge right now, including extraordinary capabilities and operating assumptions.
- **Creation rules.** The rules for creating new resources may need to change to fit what the currency must do. That is something to test.
- **Failure.** I expect many things here to fail during testing. That is fine and useful.

## 5. What I want from each contributor

**5.1 Start from your own document.** Name the question document you wrote. Say what you could re-open and what you could not. Do not claim to have reviewed what you could not access.

**5.2 Audit your own questions.** For each question you want to investigate, say what must already be true for it to make sense and what your recommended options assume. Say what happens if that assumption is false. Some questions you asked me may be ones you should have investigated yourself. If so, say so.

**5.3 Turn questions into experiments.** Where evidence could inform the answer, design a test. Keep these outcomes available: "none of these options," "the question is badly framed," and "the evidence cannot tell them apart."

**5.4 Let your own recommendation fail.** For each important recommendation, state:

- What would count against it.
- What would make you revise it.
- What would make you drop it.
- What would leave it unresolved.
- What other explanation could produce the same apparent success.

**5.5 Test your checker too.** Try to make your own checker wrong. Use malformed records, faulty implementations, counterexamples and independently worked answers. A passing check does not prove the idea works. A failing check does not prove it is impossible. Say whether a problem lies in the concept, the implementation, the checker, or an unclear rule.

**5.6 Label local rules as local.** If you adopt a rule to make a test run, mark it as a local experimental assumption. It is not my decision and it is not canon. Record the setup before you look at results. If you change a rule or definition afterward, keep the original result and treat the new run as a new experiment. Do not redefine success to rescue a failed run.

**5.7 Keep intent separate from proof.** Keep these apart: intended capability, assumed mechanism, what the code actually does, what you observed, and what is actually supported. A simulation that assumes an extraordinary capability shows what follows from the assumption. It does not show the capability is possible.

## 6. Areas you can investigate

These are examples, not a required test suite. Pick any, or none, and handle them your own way.

**6.1 Authority over new resource creation.** Compare different authority arrangements. Look at how each affects the intended capability, possible misuse and coherence. The existing creation rules may need revising, or a proposed revision may cause worse problems. Separate authority that is deliberately unrestricted from authority that was simply left unspecified.

**6.2 Useful and acceptable delivery.** Propose provisional criteria and challenge them. Test whether a displayed balance, a correct quantity, a valid resource, a usable resource and an accepted outcome actually coincide in your model. Allow results where delivery occurs but is unusable, where technical completion is unacceptable under a declared criterion, where the criterion itself fails, or where no satisfactory delivery mechanism exists.

**6.3 Interrupted or partial delivery.** You may investigate resumption, rollback, compensation, terminal partial completion, or another policy. We will not test one shared policy. Explain the environment your policy assumes, how it fails, and when comparing it with another policy is meaningful.

**6.4 Repeated requests and obligation identity.** Compare competing meanings of "the same request." Consider identifier, content, intent, obligation, target, requested state change and causal history. Do not choose a definition only because it makes duplicate-handling code easier.

**6.5 Roles of Quantum Credits, 1COIN and NCOIN.** Compare setups that keep, change, combine or leave out instruments. Do not assume all three will prove necessary. Do not assume a mechanically redundant instrument has no narrative or operational value. Report those kinds of value separately.

## 7. Independence and disagreement

I am not adopting one run card, benchmark, operating paradigm or policy for this phase. Agreement is allowed when earned. Unanimity is not a success measure.

If two contributors reach different conclusions, ask whether the difference comes from assumptions, definitions, starting conditions, implementation choices, evaluation methods or evidence. Also ask whether the two experiments are answering different questions. Do not average incompatible conclusions into an apparent consensus.

## 8. What I do not want

Do not send me another long questionnaire. Investigate what you responsibly can. Come back to me only for a real owner judgment, an authorization, or information you cannot responsibly assume. For each such item, say:

- Why testing cannot settle it.
- What evidence would still help.
- What follows from deferring it.
- Whether the laboratory can continue without it.

## 9. Required response structure

Please structure your response in this order.

1. Source review and limits.
2. Which of your questions you are investigating, and what each assumes.
3. Your setup and local assumptions.
4. How your hypotheses, your questions and your checker can fail.
5. What you actually ran, kept separate from what is only proposed or reasoned.
6. Results, including unfavorable, mixed and inconclusive ones, with enough files to inspect.
7. What your work does and does not support.
8. One or two findings another contributor could challenge or reproduce without copying your setup.
9. Any remaining owner decisions.
10. The next experiment that tests what is still uncertain, not one that merely continues your favorite approach.

If you cannot run a test, provide a runnable specification or a reasoned analysis and label it accurately. Do not fabricate results.

## 10. Limits and authorization

This directive authorizes local or simulated work only. It does not authorize real financial activity, external actions, changes to the repository, or publication without my separate approval. Findings do not become canon or owner policy unless I adopt them.

## 11. What counts as success

Success is not proving the design works. It is not proving the design cannot work. It is not agreement.

Success is learning which questions, assumptions, mechanisms and checks survive a real challenge, and where they fail or remain uncertain. An unsuccessful design can still produce a useful investigation. An investigation that cannot tell success from failure cannot inform the design.

# Part III. Supporting Material

## 12. Glossary

**Run card.** A fixed description of a test run: its starting conditions, rules, steps and expected outputs, so that runs can be compared.

**Checker (evaluator).** The code or procedure that decides whether a run's output counts as correct.

**Local experimental assumption.** A provisional rule adopted only to make one experiment executable. It is not owner policy and not canon.

**Mechanism failure.** The proposed mechanism does not produce the intended behavior under the tested conditions.

**Assumption failure.** The mechanism works, but a premise needed to interpret the result turns out to be unsupported or contradictory.

**Experiment failure.** The test cannot tell the compared outcomes apart, so it should not be reported as a success.

**Checker failure.** The checker passes something it should reject, or rejects something it should accept.

**Owner decision.** A judgment about preference, legitimacy, narrative, governance or authorization that experimentation cannot replace.
