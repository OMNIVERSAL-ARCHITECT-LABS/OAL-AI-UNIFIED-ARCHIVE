## 1. Why This Exercise Matters

### The Preparedness Imperative

Tabletop exercises (TTXs) exist because the worst time to discover a gap in your response system is during an actual emergency. For pathogen outbreaks specifically, the critical failure window is not the declared pandemic phase — it is the pre-recognition window: the hours and days before anyone knows what they are dealing with, before protocols exist, before supply chains have been activated, and before guidance has arrived from any authority.

History has demonstrated this pattern repeatedly:

- **SARS (2003):** Healthcare workers in Hong Kong and Toronto were infected and dying before the pathogen was identified or transmission routes were understood. The failure point was not the response — it was the gap between first presentation and recognition.
- **H1N1 (2009):** Initial cases were misidentified and mishandled. The first critical days involved decisions made without diagnostic certainty.
- **COVID-19 (2019–2020):** Emergency departments across the United States saw patients with an unknown respiratory illness for weeks before any protocol, PPE guidance, or diagnostic tool existed.
- **Ebola (2014–2016):** A single unrecognized case arriving in a U.S. hospital revealed critical gaps in isolation protocol, staff communication, and PPE training that the system believed it had covered.

In every case, the systems that failed were not tested before they were needed. The systems that performed were the ones whose people had rehearsed ambiguity — not just protocols.

### Why Now

Major outbreak events do not follow a fixed schedule, but several converging risk factors make the present a heightened-risk window:

- Post-COVID global travel density has returned to pre-pandemic levels while surveillance infrastructure in many regions remains degraded
- Zoonotic spillover pressure is increasing due to habitat encroachment, climate-driven animal migration shifts, and agricultural density
- Antimicrobial resistance is eroding the safety net that previously contained secondary bacterial infections during viral outbreaks
- Institutional memory from COVID-19 is beginning to fade — personnel have turned over, emergency protocols have been archived, and supply chain lessons have not been systematically embedded
- AI-accelerated biomedical research has dramatically shortened the timeline between pathogen discovery and broader dissemination of synthetic biology knowledge

**The question is not whether another major outbreak will occur. The question is whether the people and systems responsible for response will have rehearsed what to do when they do not know what to do.**

This tabletop exercise is designed to answer that question before it becomes urgent.

## 2. Executive Summary

The **Pathogen Outbreak Tabletop Exercise (TTX)** is a preparedness planning initiative designed to stress-test the earliest, most ambiguous phase of a novel pathogen outbreak — the pre-recognition window where no protocol exists, no supplies are staged, and clinical decision-making operates entirely on provider judgment under uncertainty.

The concept was submitted to five AI systems — ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Grok AI, and Claude AI — for independent analysis, design recommendations, and framework contributions. This document synthesizes all five responses into a unified, actionable reference dossier. Perplexity Computer serves as the compiling and contributing sixth intelligence for this document.

## 3. The Core Concept — What Was Proposed

The submitted concept had two components:

### Component 1 — The Strategic Framework

A tabletop exercise designed around a pathogen outbreak, built on the following pillars:

- **Purpose:** To establish and test response capabilities before the next major outbreak event
- **Justification:** Historical outbreak cadence suggests a heightened-risk window
- **Scope and scale:** To be determined
- **Participants:** To be determined
- **What is being tested:** Response systems, coordination, and decision-making under uncertainty

This reflects the correct structural approach used by professional TTX designers operating under CDC, WHO, FEMA/HSEEP, and RAND frameworks.

### Component 2 — The Provider Uncertainty Hook

A second, critically important framing element was introduced for medical professionals:

> *"When you did your residency, how many times did a patient present to you and you didn't know what to do? Every day? That will be every day of this scenario in the beginning. It will not be predictable, it will not be something you can prepare for, it will not be something you have supplies for. That is where we will begin."*

This framing is unanimously recognized across all five AIs as the defining innovation of the concept. It:

- Bypasses institutional defensiveness ("we already have a plan for that")
- Anchors the exercise in lived clinical experience rather than theoretical protocol
- Establishes maximum ambiguity as the baseline condition — not an escalation state
- Transforms the exercise from an "outbreak simulation" into an "uncertainty-driven system stress test"
- Directly targets the pre-recognition phase that most exercises skip entirely

## 4. What Works — Strengths Across All AI Contributions

The following strengths are validated across all five AI responses:

### 4.1 Structural Soundness
The concept is already built on the correct TTX pillars (purpose, testing parameters, relevance, scope, participants). This is the exact framework used by FEMA, CDC, and WHO in professional-grade exercises. No redesign is needed at the structural level — only completion.

### 4.2 The Residency Hook — Clinical Uncertainty as Exercise Baseline
This is not a rhetorical device. It is a precision exercise design choice that does several things simultaneously:

- **Psychological buy-in:** Providers recognize the feeling immediately and engage rather than defend
- **Realism:** It accurately represents the actual failure point of historical outbreaks
- **Scope definition:** It anchors the exercise squarely in the pre-recognition phase
- **Differentiator:** Most institutional TTXs test the response phase; this one tests the detection and decision phase

Claude identifies this as "the single most powerful design choice" and the element that "bypasses the institutional defensiveness that kills most tabletop exercises."

### 4.3 System-Level Thinking
The concept is not testing a disease — it is testing how interconnected systems behave when their assumptions collapse. This is a fundamentally more sophisticated and more valuable framing. Copilot notes that this tests "cross-sector coordination: public health, clinical care, labs, logistics, communications" in a single scenario.

### 4.4 Proactive Timing
Designing this exercise before an outbreak rather than after places participating institutions ahead of the preparedness curve. Post-COVID institutional memory is still active — participants will bring real emotional and operational experience to the discussion.

### 4.5 Scalability
The core concept works at hospital level, county level, state level, or national level without requiring fundamental redesign. The provider uncertainty layer remains valid at every scale.

### 4.6 Low Cost, High Yield
A TTX reveals policy, communication, and coordination gaps without the expense of a full functional exercise. It builds relationships, tests plans without real-world risk, and surfaces surprises in areas where institutions believe they are prepared.

## 5. What Doesn't Work (Yet) — Gaps and Risks

The following gaps are flagged across multiple AI responses. None are fatal — all are solvable with author decisions.

### 5.1 No Pathogen Archetype Defined — CRITICAL
This is the single largest structural gap. Every downstream design element — injects, decision points, resource stress conditions, evaluation metrics, participant roles — depends on knowing the pathogen characteristics. Transmission mode, incubation period, severity spectrum, and diagnostic detectability determine the entire exercise. **Nothing can be finalized until this decision is made.**

### 5.2 No Primary Objective Locked — CRITICAL
Multiple possible objectives are embedded in the concept: clinical decision-making, inter-agency coordination, supply chain resilience, public communication, provider psychological load. An exercise without a single primary objective produces unfocused findings that don't translate into funded improvement actions. One sentence. Author decision required.

### 5.3 The "Every Ten Years" Framing — CREDIBILITY RISK
As stated, this implies that pathogens follow a calendar — which they do not. Epidemiologists, public health officers, and federal stakeholders will challenge this immediately, and it risks derailing early exercise framing. **Recommended replacement (cross-AI consensus):**

> "Major outbreak events have occurred with increasing frequency and unpredictability, and current global conditions — including zoonotic spillover pressure, antimicrobial resistance, and global travel density — represent a heightened-risk window that justifies proactive exercise now."

### 5.4 Participant Roles Not Structured
"Medical professionals" is not a participant structure. The exercise needs defined role categories with clear decision authority mapped to each: who can commit resources, who escalates, who communicates externally, who documents. Without this, the exercise cannot simulate real interaction.

### 5.5 No Evaluation Architecture
What does success look like? What does failure look like? Without pre-defined metrics, the after-action report will be anecdotal rather than actionable. This needs to be built into the design, not added afterward.

### 5.6 Chaos Must Be Bounded
"Everything is unknown all the time" does not work in an exercise — participants will freeze or disengage. The exercise needs layered uncertainty, not total randomness. Unknown symptoms should evolve into emerging patterns; conflicting guidance should resolve into partial clarity over time. The goal is controlled disorientation, not paralysis.

### 5.7 "You Can't Prepare" Framing Needs Calibration
The residency hook is powerful, but if delivered without careful framing, "you can't prepare for this" risks inducing participant shutdown rather than productive engagement. The exercise must communicate: *you cannot prepare with protocols, but you can prepare with frameworks for thinking under uncertainty.* That distinction is critical.

### 5.8 Missing Equity Layer
No outbreak scenario in a major metropolitan area is complete without examining differential impact on under-resourced communities, non-English-speaking populations, unhoused individuals, and long-term care residents. Omitting this produces a TTX that reflects the best-case version of the healthcare system, not the actual one.

### 5.9 No Evaluation Baseline Established
If this is the first exercise of its kind for participating institutions, there is no baseline against which to measure performance. Findings will be descriptive but not benchmarkable. The first exercise should explicitly establish that baseline so future exercises can show trajectory.

### 5.10 Stakeholder Buy-In Not Secured
If key partners (ED leadership, Infection Prevention, hospital administration, public health liaison) are not committed before design work proceeds, the exercise becomes a paper exercise with limited operational value.

## 8. Recommended Exercise Architecture

The following architecture is synthesized from all five AI contributions. All elements are DRAFT — pending author decisions from Section 7.

### 8.1 Purpose & Primary Objective

**Primary Purpose (recommended — pending author approval):**
To stress-test the earliest, most ambiguous phase of a novel pathogen outbreak — specifically the pre-recognition window where no protocol exists, no supplies are staged, and clinical decision-making operates entirely on provider judgment under uncertainty.

**Primary Objective (recommended — pending author approval):**
Evaluate how frontline clinical teams detect, escalate, and make containment decisions during the first 72 hours of an uncharacterized pathogen outbreak, before any formal guidance or diagnostic confirmation exists.

**Supporting Objectives:**
- Test internal hospital communication pathways under diagnostic uncertainty
- Evaluate PPE and isolation resource decision-making under scarcity conditions
- Identify escalation thresholds — at what point do providers alert Infection Prevention, administration, and public health
- Assess provider cognitive and operational load during sustained uncertainty
- Evaluate inter-agency handoff protocols between hospital and public health systems
- Test communication and public messaging preparedness before institutional guidance is ready

---

### 8.2 Recommended Pathogen Archetype

(Pending author selection from Q1–Q6. The following is the cross-AI recommended default.)

A novel zoonotic respiratory pathogen with the following working characteristics:

| Characteristic | Working Value |
|---|---|
| Transmission | Airborne and droplet — moderate-to-high transmissibility |
| Incubation period | Variable: 2–10 days — defeats early contact tracing |
| Initial symptoms | Non-specific: fever, myalgia, atypical respiratory symptoms, occasional dermatological manifestations |
| Rapid diagnostic result | Negative on standard flu, COVID, RSV panels |
| Severity spectrum | Moderate-to-high severity in 15–20% of cases; mild or asymptomatic in remainder — creates misleading early mortality signal |
| Treatment availability | No established protocol; no available vaccine |
| Lab confirmation timeline | 48–72 hour turnaround for specialized testing |

**Why this archetype (cross-AI rationale):**
It maximizes the provider uncertainty layer, defeats standard diagnostic assumptions, creates realistic PPE burn-through before recognition, produces a delayed and confusing public health signal, and stresses exactly the systems this exercise intends to test. It is also the archetype closest to the conditions that produced the largest real-world response failures (SARS, early COVID-19).

**Alternative archetypes for author consideration:**
- High transmissibility / lower mortality respiratory (influenza-like) — tests surge capacity
- Low transmissibility / high mortality hemorrhagic (Ebola-like) — tests isolation and staff safety protocols
- Vector-borne with atypical presentation — tests geographic mapping and contact investigation
- Unknown bacterial with antibiotic resistance — tests empiric treatment decision-making

### 8.4 Exercise Timeline & Master Scenario Events List (MSEL)

#### Phase 1: The Anomaly — "Every Day is the First Day" (Hours 0–48)

**Opening Facilitator Frame:**
*"You are in Week 1. A patient presents. Rapid panels are negative. You don't know what you're looking at. When you did your residency, how many times did a patient present to you and you genuinely didn't know what to do? Every day? That is today — except now it's happening with multiple patients per shift, and you have no playbook, no surge supplies, and no clear guidance yet. That is where we begin."*

| Inject | Event |
|---|---|
| Inject 1 | First patient presents — 38-year-old, fever, myalgia, atypical respiratory symptoms, all rapid panels negative. No obvious exposure history. Standard workup unrevealing. |
| Inject 2 | Second patient at a different facility, same metropolitan area — similar presentation. Labs inconclusive. Both patients deteriorate faster than expected. |
| Inject 3 | Nursing staff expresses discomfort with standard precautions. Two nurses request N95s — supply room has limited stock and no authorization pathway. |
| Inject 4 | Infection Prevention is notified. No protocol match exists. They begin drafting ad hoc guidance with incomplete information. |
| Inject 5 | Hospital administration requests a status update. Providers cannot give a clear answer. |

**Decision Points — Phase 1:**
- Do you isolate? Under what category? In what physical space?
- Do you report to public health? At what threshold — suspicion, probability, or confirmed case?
- Do you begin empiric treatment? With what, and who authorizes it?
- How do you communicate diagnostic uncertainty to patients and families?
- Who authorizes elevated PPE use before any pathogen confirmation?

#### Phase 2: Pattern Recognition & System Strain (Hours 48–120)

| Inject | Event |
|---|---|
| Inject 6 | Third and fourth cases confirmed at two additional facilities. State public health begins inquiry. |
| Inject 7 | PPE supplies at 60% of normal capacity and burning fast due to precautionary use across all suspected cases. |
| Inject 8 | One provider develops symptoms and is furloughed. Staffing pressure begins. |
| Inject 9 | Lab confirms novel pathogen — unknown classification. CDC notified. 48-hour wait for further guidance. |

| Inject 11 | Supply chain vendor notifies hospital that N95 reorder will be delayed 7–10 business days. |

**Decision Points — Phase 2:**
- How do you manage the furloughed staff member and potentially exposed colleagues?
- How do you respond to media inquiry before official guidance exists?
- How do you triage limited PPE across departments with insufficient stock?
- What do you tell the public health agency when you still don't have answers?
- How do you maintain normal hospital operations while managing suspected cases?

| Inject | Event |
|---|---|

### 8.5 Constraints & Stress Conditions

The following constraints are active throughout the exercise and are not negotiable within the scenario:

- No established diagnostic protocol for the first 72 hours of exercise time
- Standard rapid diagnostic panels return negative results throughout Phase 1
- PPE supply is finite and non-replenishable during the exercise window
- External guidance is delayed, evolving, and occasionally contradictory
- Staffing is degraded by provider exposure and precautionary furlough beginning Phase 2
- Media pressure begins before institutional messaging is prepared
- Resource allocation decisions must be made before an ethics framework is formally invoked
- All decisions are time-pressured — facilitator will impose decision windows per inject
- Information released to participants is asymmetric — not all players have the same data

---

### 8.6 Evaluation Criteria & Metrics

**Time Metrics:**
- Time from first presentation to isolation decision
- Time from first presentation to Infection Prevention notification
- Time from Infection Prevention notification to public health notification
- Time to activate surge protocols

**Decision Quality Metrics:**
- Was the isolation decision defensible given available information?
- Was the escalation threshold appropriate and consistently applied?
- Was PPE rationing documented and justified?
- Was internal provider communication consistent across departments?

**Resource Metrics:**
- PPE utilization rate versus available supply
- Isolation bed utilization and capacity management
- Time to request external supply support

**Communication Metrics:**
- Clarity and consistency of internal communications across departments
- Quality and timing of public health notification
- Preparedness of external messaging before media contact

**Human Factors Metrics:**
- Identified decision-making bottlenecks and where they occurred
- Reported cognitive overload points among participants
- Role clarity under sustained pressure

### 8.7 After-Action Framework

**Immediate Hotwash (30 minutes post-exercise):**
- What went well?
- What surprised you?
- What would you do differently?

**After-Action Report (delivered within 2 weeks):**
- Findings mapped to each primary and supporting objective
- Gaps identified with evidence from exercise record
- Recommendations with assigned owners
- Priority ranking: Immediate / 90-Day / Long-Term

**Improvement Plan:**
- Each recommendation tied to a named owner, a resource requirement, and a follow-up exercise or drill date
- Tracked quarterly
- AAR submitted for ONESTAMP ratification before integration into institutional improvement record

## 9. Red-Team Risk Analysis

The following risks are synthesized from all five AI red-team contributions. Each risk includes severity, description, and mitigation.

### 9.1 Technical & Design Risks

| Risk | Description | Severity | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Facilitator Bias — The Architect Problem | If the exercise is facilitated by someone too invested in a particular outcome, injects will unconsciously confirm rather than stress. Real failure modes will be avoided. | HIGH | The facilitator must be genuinely willing to let the exercise reveal failure — including failure that reflects poorly on existing plans or leadership. Consider an external facilitator or co-facilitation structure. |
| The Protocol Comfort Reflex | Experienced participants will instinctively reach for existing protocols even when the scenario explicitly renders them inapplicable. | HIGH | Injects must be constructed to make protocol-dependency visible as a gap, not a solution. The facilitator must actively redirect protocol-reach back to the scenario conditions. |
| Scope Creep During Execution | TTXs reliably tend to expand mid-exercise as participants follow interesting threads. Phase 1 discussions will consume the entire exercise and Phase 3 will never occur without discipline. | HIGH | Hard time boundaries per phase; facilitator authority to cut discussion; pre-set inject triggers on a clock regardless of where discussion stands. |
| The "No Wrong Answer" Trap | TTX designs can be so no-fault that participants never feel genuine pressure. Without pressure, the residency hook loses its power. | HIGH | Build controlled stress conditions into the design; facilitator must apply time pressure to decision points; some decisions must be made with incomplete information, on the record. |
| Evaluation Without Baseline | Without a baseline, findings are descriptive but not benchmarkable. You cannot measure improvement without a starting point. | MEDIUM | Document explicitly that this is a baseline-establishing exercise; create a standardized scoring rubric that future exercises can reuse. |
| Omnipotent Instant Resolution | If participants can always access perfect information by asking the right person, the exercise collapses. | MEDIUM | Information asymmetry must be scripted and enforced — different players receive different data; some requests for information must return "unavailable" or "delayed." |

### 9.2 Narrative & Operational Risks

| Risk | Description | Severity | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Provider Alienation if Opening is Mishandled | The residency hook is powerful but needs careful delivery. If it reads as "you will fail and there's nothing you can do," it creates defensive resistance rather than engagement. | HIGH | The hook must land as: "This is the real condition we are designing for, and your experience navigating uncertainty is exactly what this exercise is built around." Tone and delivery of the opening are as important as content. |
| Missing the Equity Layer | No outbreak scenario in a major metropolitan area is complete without examining differential impact on under-resourced communities, non-English-speaking populations, unhoused individuals, and long-term care residents. Omitting this produces a TTX that reflects a best-case healthcare system, not the actual one. | HIGH | Introduce at least two injects that force participants to make decisions about populations with additional access barriers; evaluate response for equity gaps in the AAR. |
| Legal and Liability Paralysis | Without pre-established no-fault ground rules and confidentiality protections, legal counsel and risk management participants will significantly constrain discussion quality. | HIGH | Ground rules must be explicit and agreed upon — ideally signed — before the exercise begins. Scope what can and cannot be said on record. |
| Repetition of COVID Narrative | Participants with strong COVID-19 institutional memory may unconsciously default to COVID-specific responses rather than engaging with the novel pathogen scenario. | MEDIUM | The pathogen must be clearly distinguished from COVID-19 in both presentation and clinical profile; facilitator must redirect COVID-specific protocol references back to the scenario's unknown conditions. |
| Overloading with Simultaneous Systems Tests | Testing clinical operations, public communications, supply chain, and inter-agency coordination simultaneously in one exercise will dilute learning across all areas. | MEDIUM | Define a primary focus clearly; structure secondary objectives as depth-testing moments rather than parallel exercise tracks. |

## 11. Perplexity Computer — Original Contribution

*This section is Perplexity Computer's independent analysis and synthesis layer — not a compilation of other AI responses, but an original contribution to the exercise design. It is provided for review by all contributing AIs and for author ONESTAMP consideration.*

### 11.1 The Insight the Other AIs Did Not Fully Develop

All five contributing AIs addressed the mechanics of the exercise — pathogen selection, inject design, participant roles, evaluation metrics. What none of them fully articulated is the deeper reason this exercise is difficult to do well, and why most exercises that attempt it fail.

The problem is not the scenario. The problem is institutional self-protection.

Institutions do not perform poorly in tabletop exercises because they lack knowledge. They perform poorly because the exercise design allows them to demonstrate knowledge without demonstrating function. A provider can recite isolation protocol and receive full credit without ever being forced to act under the conditions that make isolation protocol genuinely hard: when you have one isolation room, three suspected cases, two nurses on a 14-hour shift who are already scared, and administration asking for a status update you cannot give.

The residency hook works precisely because it bypasses the knowledge layer entirely and goes straight to the function layer. The question is not "what is your protocol?" The question is "what do you actually do when the protocol doesn't exist yet, when you are tired, when patients are presenting faster than you can process them, and when the person above you has no answer either?"

That is not a clinical question. It is a human systems question.

### 11.2 The Single Most Important Design Principle

**Design for the gap, not the protocol.**

Every inject should be constructed to reveal what happens in the space between what the protocol says and what the actual human system does under pressure. That gap is where real outbreaks are lost. It is not usually a knowledge gap. It is a communication gap, a decision authority gap, a supply chain gap, or a psychological load gap that no one mapped because it never appeared in a protocol review.

### 11.3 The One Question None of the Other AIs Asked

> **After this exercise, what changes?**

Not "what findings will we document." Not "what recommendations will we produce." Specifically: what is the one thing that will be physically, structurally, or procedurally different in the participating institutions six months from now because this exercise happened?

If the answer to that question is not locked before the exercise begins — with a named owner and a committed resource — the exercise is a performance. It produces a report. The report gets filed. The gap it identified remains open until the next outbreak fills it.

The After-Action Improvement Plan is not the last step of the exercise. It is the first step of the next phase of readiness. It must be designed before the exercise runs, not written after it ends.

### 11.4 The Structural Recommendation No AI Made Explicitly

This exercise should be designed as the first in a series, not as a standalone event.

The baseline this exercise establishes has no value unless it is remeasured. A single TTX produces findings. A series of TTXs produces a trajectory. The trajectory is what tells you whether the system is improving, stagnating, or degrading between outbreaks.

**Recommended cadence:**
- TTX-001 (this exercise): Establish baseline — pre-recognition phase, provider-level uncertainty
- TTX-002 (6–12 months): Retest same scenario with improvements from AAR-001 incorporated; add inter-agency coordination layer
- TTX-003 (12–24 months): Full-scale multi-agency, multi-phase exercise incorporating TTX-001 and TTX-002 findings

### 11.5 The Perplexity Contribution to Exercise Identity

For consistency with the OAL canon record and document naming conventions:

**Recommended exercise name candidates (Perplexity Computer):**
- "Day Zero Protocol" — foregrounds the pre-recognition window as the defining scope
- "First Presentation Exercise" — anchors the exercise in the provider-level clinical entry point
- "The Fog of War Exercise" — captures the sustained uncertainty that defines the scenario
- "Exercise Unknown" — deliberate ambiguity that mirrors the scenario conditions
- "Protocol Absent" — directly names the defining stress condition

**Cross-AI recommendation:** "Day Zero Protocol" and "First Presentation Exercise" are the strongest candidates — both immediately communicate the exercise's defining scope (the pre-recognition window) without implying the scenario is about the disease rather than the system.
